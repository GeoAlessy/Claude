## 0:50 – 4:00 · CAPÍTULO 1 — Why the years speed up

`[VISUAL: rótulo de capítulo minimalista: "01 · THE SHRINKING YEAR"]`

**NARRACIÓN:**
Let's start with a simple test. Pause the video if you need to — but I want you to try this.

Close your eyes. Count to thirty in your head, at what feels like one second per beat. Then open them.

`[VISUAL: un cronómetro gigante aparece en pantalla y corre 30 segundos reales mientras suena una pulsación suave. Texto: "Close your eyes. Count to 30."]`

`[VISUAL: el cronómetro se detiene en 0:30. Texto: "Where were you?"]`

Were you early? Late? Keep that in mind, because in the 1990s a researcher named James Mangan ran a version of this test with three groups: people in their early twenties, middle-aged adults, and people in their sixties. He asked them to count off three minutes.

The young group was remarkably accurate. The middle-aged group ran over. And the oldest group — on average — said "three minutes" when more than three and a half minutes had actually passed.

`[VISUAL: tres barras animadas por código creciendo: 20s ≈ 3:00 · 45–50 ≈ 3:16 · 60+ ≈ 3:40. Ficha del estudio con fuente.]`

Their internal clocks were running slow. Which means, from the inside, the outside world was running *fast*.

It was a small study, so we shouldn't lean on it too hard. But it fits a much bigger pattern.

In 2005, psychologists Marc Wittmann and Sandra Lehnhoff asked hundreds of people a simple question: how fast did the last ten years go by? The older people were, the more they felt the decade had flown — a feeling that grew through adulthood and then seemed to level off in later life.

`[VISUAL: curva animada "speed of the last decade" subiendo con la edad y aplanándose al final.]`

And you don't need a lab to see it. Ask anyone over forty, and they'll tell you the same thing: the days are long, but the years are short.

For more than a century, scientists have tried to explain why — and there are two main ideas.

The first is almost mathematical. In 1877, a French philosopher named Paul Janet pointed out that when you're ten, one year is a tenth of your entire life. When you're fifty, it's a fiftieth. Each new year is a smaller slice of everything you've lived — so it *feels* smaller.

`[VISUAL: un círculo-tarta de "vida" que se divide en cada vez más porciones; la porción del "año actual" se vuelve diminuta.]`

But the second explanation is the one that should worry you. 🔓 *"…the one that should worry you."*

## 4:00 – 7:30 · CAPÍTULO 2 — Your brain stops recording

`[VISUAL: "02 · THE BLANK PAGES"]`

**NARRACIÓN:**
Your brain doesn't have a clock that ticks off your life. When you look back and ask "how long did that take?", your brain doesn't check a timer. It checks your memories.

Lots of new, distinct memories? That period of time feels long. Few memories? It feels short.

`[VISUAL: cerebro representado como una galaxia oscura. Cada recuerdo nuevo es una estrella que se enciende. Un niño: miles de estrellas. Un adulto en rutina: casi nada se enciende.]`

Now think about being a child. Almost everything was a first. Your first day of school. The first time you saw the ocean. The first snow. Your brain was flooded with new information, and it recorded almost all of it.

Now think about an ordinary week as an adult. Same commute. Same desk. Same dinner. Same scroll before bed.

Your brain is efficient. When something is familiar, it doesn't bother storing it in detail — why would it? It's already seen that hallway a thousand times.

`[VISUAL: escena IA: oficina gris, la misma persona en el mismo escritorio; los días se superponen como capas de papel transparente que terminan siendo una sola hoja.]`

So when you look back on a routine year, your brain searches for memories… and finds blank pages. Weeks that collapsed into a single, blurry average.

That's the unsettling part. You did live those days. Your brain just decided they weren't worth keeping.

You can even see this in what people remember. Ask older adults to recall the memories that matter most in their lives, and something odd shows up: a huge cluster of them comes from roughly the ages of ten to thirty. Psychologists call it the reminiscence bump.

`[VISUAL: gráfica por código del "reminiscence bump": recuerdos por edad con una montaña entre los 10 y 30 años, y un valle plano después.]`

Why that window? Because that's when life is full of firsts — first love, first job, first apartment, first time living on your own. Novelty, everywhere. After that, for many of us, life settles into patterns. And the memories thin out.

Think about your first week at a new job. It felt enormous, didn't it? A month later, the same office, the same tasks — and the weeks started slipping by. Nothing about the clock changed. Only how much your brain was writing down.

✅ *(cierra "the one that should worry you")*

And if routine makes time vanish, you'd expect the opposite to be true: that terror — the most intense experience possible — would stretch time out.

In the next experiment, scientists tested that by dropping people from a tower. 🔓

## 7:30 – 10:30 · CAPÍTULO 3 — The slow-motion illusion

`[VISUAL: "03 · THE FALL"]`

**NARRACIÓN:**
Almost everyone who's been in a car crash says the same thing: everything went into slow motion. The glass, the spinning, the sound — all stretched out.

So is your brain actually speeding up in an emergency, like a high-speed camera?

`[VISUAL: escena IA en cámara lenta: el interior de un coche, cristales suspendidos en el aire.]`

In 2007, neuroscientist David Eagleman and his team decided to find out. They took volunteers to an amusement park ride called SCAD — a tower where you're dropped backwards, in free fall, for about thirty meters into a net.

`[VISUAL: animación por código del experimento: torre, caída libre de ~3 segundos, red. Contador de altura bajando.]`

First, they asked people how long their own fall felt. On average, they estimated it lasted about a third longer than other people's falls they had watched.

Then came the clever part. Each volunteer wore a small device on their wrist that flashed numbers just slightly too fast to read under normal conditions. If the brain really sped up during fear, people should suddenly be able to read them in mid-air.

`[VISUAL: recreación del "cronómetro perceptivo" de muñeca: dígitos parpadeando demasiado rápido para leerse.]`

They couldn't. Not one of them.

So your brain does *not* see the world in slow motion when you're terrified. Something stranger happens.

During fear, a region called the amygdala kicks in and makes your brain record memories in extreme detail — much denser than normal. Later, when you replay the event, there are so many memories packed into those few seconds that your brain concludes: *this must have taken a long time.*

`[VISUAL: la galaxia-cerebro: durante la caída se encienden cientos de estrellas en un instante; luego la línea de tiempo de esos 3 segundos se estira como un chicle.]`

The slow motion isn't happening during the moment. It's created afterwards — in memory.

And you don't need to fall from a tower to feel a smaller version of this. In lab experiments, when people watch a series of identical images and suddenly one different image appears — an "oddball" — they judge that unexpected image as lasting longer than the others, even when it was on screen for exactly the same time.

`[VISUAL: secuencia de círculos grises idénticos; de pronto aparece uno rojo. Texto: "Which one lasted longer?" Todos duraron lo mismo.]`

Surprise stretches time. Familiarity shrinks it. It's the same rule, over and over: the more your brain has to process, the more time it believes has passed.

And that brings us to the place where your brain invents time completely from nothing: your dreams. 🔓

## 10:30 – 13:00 · CAPÍTULO 4 — Time inside your dreams

`[VISUAL: "04 · THE DREAM CLOCK"]`

**NARRACIÓN:**
You've probably heard this one: a dream that feels like it lasted hours actually takes only a few seconds. It's one of the most repeated "facts" about sleep.

It's probably wrong.

`[VISUAL: dormitorio oscuro en IA. Una persona durmiendo; sobre ella flota un sueño en espiral.]`

How do you time a dream? You can't ask someone mid-dream. Unless… they're lucid — aware that they're dreaming.

In the 1980s, sleep researcher Stephen LaBerge found a way in. During REM sleep your body is paralyzed, but your eyes still move. So he trained lucid dreamers to send a signal from inside the dream: look left-right-left-right, count to ten in the dream, then signal again. The movements showed up on the lab's sensors — a message from inside a dream, in real time.

`[VISUAL: gráfica de movimiento ocular por código: dos picos de señal separados por ~10 segundos reales. Texto: "Signal" … "Signal".]`

The result? Counting in a dream took roughly the same time as counting while awake. Later studies found some actions, like physical exercise in a dream, can take a bit longer — but nothing like the hours-in-seconds myth.

So why do dreams *feel* so long? Because your brain jumps between scenes without transitions — a beach, then a school, then your old house — and when you wake up, you remember several "chapters." Your memory stitches them together and assumes a lot of time must have passed.

`[VISUAL: escenas de sueño encadenadas con cortes bruscos; al final se ordenan en una línea que parece larguísima.]`

Same trick as the fall. More memories, more time — whether or not it really happened.

There's one more strange detail. Lucid dreamers often say that clocks and text behave badly in dreams. You look at a clock once, look away, look back — and the time has changed, or the numbers make no sense. Many people even use it as a test: if the clock won't stay still, you're dreaming.

`[VISUAL: reloj de sueño cuyos números se deforman y cambian al volver a mirarlo.]`

In a sense, that makes perfect sense. In a dream there is no outside world to keep the time. Your brain is generating everything — including the clock. It's the purest version of what it does all day: building time instead of measuring it.

But the strangest lie of all is happening right now, while you're watching this. 🔓

## 13:00 – 15:30 · CAPÍTULO 5 — You live in the past

`[VISUAL: "05 · THE DELAY"]`

**NARRACIÓN:**
Here's another test. If there's a clock with a second hand near you — or one on your phone — glance at it quickly.

Did the first second seem to last a little longer than the next ones? As if the clock froze for a moment?

`[VISUAL: un reloj analógico generado por código ocupa la pantalla; la primera "salta" del segundero se percibe más larga. Texto: "Look at the second hand."]`

That's a real, measured illusion called chronostasis — the "stopped clock" illusion. When your eyes jump to something new, there's a split second where you're essentially blind during the movement. Your brain doesn't show you that gap. Instead, it fills it in with the image of whatever your eyes landed on — and backdates it.

In other words, your brain edits the timeline of what you see. Constantly.

`[VISUAL: animación: el ojo salta de un punto a otro; el trayecto del salto aparece como un "hueco negro" que el cerebro rellena con una copia de la imagen final.]`

Your eyes make these quick jumps several times every second. Add up all those tiny blackouts, and by some estimates you spend a surprising amount of every waking day functionally blind — and you never notice, because your brain hides every gap.

And there's more. Signals from your eyes, ears and skin don't reach your brain at the same speed. To stitch them into one smooth reality, your brain waits — collecting information for a fraction of a second before deciding what "now" is.

`[VISUAL: tres líneas de señal (vista, oído, tacto) llegando con retrasos distintos a una "puerta" que se abre solo cuando todas llegan.]`

Which means the "present" you experience is a reconstruction of something that already happened, assembled a fraction of a second too late. You have never, not once, seen the present moment.

You can catch this delay with an illusion called the flash-lag effect. When a dot moves smoothly across a screen and a light flashes right next to it, people consistently see the moving dot *ahead* of the flash — even when they were perfectly lined up.

`[VISUAL: prueba en pantalla generada por código: un punto se mueve en línea recta y un destello aparece alineado con él; el espectador lo verá "por detrás". Se repite dos veces y luego se congela para mostrar que estaban alineados.]`

Did you see it? The dot looked like it was already past the flash. Researchers like David Eagleman have argued that this happens because your brain doesn't decide what happened at a moment until it has gathered what comes just *after* it. Your perception of "now" is written with a little information from the future.

So your brain isn't a live broadcast. It's a recorded show, edited slightly after the fact — and presented to you as if it were happening live.

So if your brain controls time this completely… can you take control back? 🔓 *(conecta con el bucle principal)*

## 15:30 – 18:30 · CAPÍTULO 6 — How to make your life feel longer

`[VISUAL: "06 · TAKING TIME BACK"]`

**NARRACIÓN:**
Let's go back to where we started. Remember: your brain measures your life by the memories it keeps — not by the clock.

That gives us a strange but powerful idea. If routine makes years disappear, then novelty should bring them back.

The writer and psychologist Claudia Hammond calls this the holiday paradox. On a trip to a new place, the days fly by — but when you get home, that one week feels enormous in memory, longer than months of ordinary life. Boredom works the opposite way: an hour in a waiting room drags forever, then vanishes from your memory almost completely.

`[VISUAL: dos líneas de tiempo lado a lado: "Vacation" (rápida al vivirla, enorme al recordarla) vs "Routine" (lenta al vivirla, casi invisible al recordarla).]`

So how do you use this?

Not with big expensive trips. With small new firsts. Take a different route home. Cook something you've never cooked. Learn a skill. Go somewhere new on a weekend — even a nearby town. Change the scenery of your ordinary days.

`[VISUAL: la galaxia-cerebro vuelve a encenderse estrella a estrella con cada "primera vez".]`

There's a reason this works. Remember the fall from the tower, and the oddball image: whenever your brain has to process something new, it writes more down. You're not cheating time. You're giving your brain a reason to keep the pages.

`[VISUAL: un diario cuyas páginas en blanco se van llenando de escenas pequeñas: una calle nueva, una receta, una vista desde una colina.]`

Some people go further and keep a tiny daily record — a single sentence, or one second of video every day. Not to share it. Just to give each day a handle their memory can grab later. When they look back on the year, it doesn't collapse into one blur. It has shape.

And pay attention. Your brain records what you notice. Ten seconds of really looking at a sunset, the face of someone you love, the street you walk every day — that's a memory with an anchor. And anchors are what make a year feel full instead of empty.

You can't stop time. But you can stop your brain from deleting it.
